# Where to start

**If you opened DuoBalance, saw a blank screen and had no idea what to do — you're not alone, and it's not your fault.**

No one taught us this. Not at school, not at university, not at home. Many of us have degrees, master's, good jobs, and still no one ever explained how to keep a month's money in order. So you open a finance app and feel the same as walking into a gym for the first time: you know you should do something, but you don't know what, and for fear of doing it wrong, you do nothing.

This guide is here to get you unstuck. Five minutes, and by the end you'll know exactly what to do today.

---

## What personal finance really answers

It all comes down to three questions:

1. **Where does my money go?**
2. **Does more come in than goes out?**
3. **What do I want to change?**
   Nothing else. Everything else — budgets, goals, saving, investing — comes from answering these three, **in that order**.

And that's the most common mistake: almost everyone starts with number three.

---

## Rule number one: track first, budget later

Most people think "getting finances in order" means making a budget. They sit down to decide how much they'll spend on food next month, realize they have no idea, get frustrated, and that's where the attempt ends.

**You can't budget what you haven't measured.** A budget made without data is a wish, and wishes break after ten days.

It's the same as the gym or a diet: no one starts by deciding how much body fat they'll lose in March. First you step on the scale, first you write down what you eat. Measuring doesn't change anything on its own — but it's the only thing that tells you the truth, and without the truth no plan works.

So for now:

- **Balances** → you will use this from day one
- **Budget** → ignore it for two to four weeks
- **Bills** → add only what you already know you pay every month
  We'll come back to budgeting once you have real numbers.

---

## What to track

**Everything that comes in and everything that goes out.** No exceptions, no filtering.

Yes, that $1.50 coffee, the bus fare, the phone top-up too. Especially those. You already have the big expenses in your head: you know how much you pay in rent. The ones you don't see are the ones that quietly take your money, and they're exactly the ones we never write down because they seem insignificant.

For each movement you only need three things:

- **How much**: the amount
- **When**: the date
- **What kind**: a category, even if approximate

That's it. You don't need the store name, a note, or a photo of the receipt. The app is built so this takes less than five seconds, and that speed is what keeps you doing it tomorrow.

---

## Use few categories

This is the mistake most people make: they create thirty categories on day one. Then they buy something at the supermarket and get stuck wondering if it's "Food", "Home" or "Groceries" — and that moment of doubt, repeated, is exactly what makes people quit.

**Start with five to eight. Nothing more.** Something like:

- Food
- Transport
- Housing (rent, electricity, water, internet)
- Health
- Personal (clothes, haircuts, treats)
- Fun
- Other
  When in doubt, put the expense in whichever comes to mind first and move on. In a month you'll see which categories actually work for you and you can adjust. It's much easier to split a big category later than to choose between thirty today.

## Permission to do it imperfectly

Read this twice, because it's what most people need to hear:

- **You don't have to start on the 1st of the month.** Start today, even if it's the 17th.
- **You don't have to know your exact balance.** Put an approximate number and fix it later.
- **If you forget to log for two days, you didn't fail — you're just two days behind.** Don't delete anything, don't start over.
- **No one will grade your categories.** There is no single right answer.
  An incomplete but real record is infinitely more valuable than a perfect system you abandoned in week two.

## Your 7-day plan

A short commitment with a clear end.

**Today (10 minutes)**

1. Create your accounts: cash, bank account, card. Give each one the approximate balance it has right now.
2. Log **one** expense. Any one. The last thing you bought. This is the step that matters — the rest is habit.
3. If you know you pay for something every month (rent, electricity, internet), add it under Bills.
   **Days 1 to 7 (30 seconds a day)**

Log everything that comes in and goes out, the same day. Pick a fixed moment: when you get home, or before bed. Always the same time.

**Day 7 (5 minutes)**

Look at your totals. Don't judge anything yet, just look. Almost everyone has the same reaction: _"I didn't know I spent so much on that."_

That sentence is the goal for week one. Nothing more.

## If you're doing this as a couple

This is where this app is different, and where it can also get complicated. Three agreements before you start:

**1. Define what's shared and what's personal.** Rent and groceries are shared. The gift one buys for the other is not. The app lets you have private accounts for exactly that: not everything has to be visible for the household books to add up.

**2. This is not for auditing each other.** The moment one starts reviewing the other's expenses to complain, the tool stops helping and becomes a new problem. The numbers belong to the household, they're not evidence.

**3. One conversation a month, not an argument per purchase.** Pick a day — the first Sunday, for example — to look at the totals together for ten minutes. That's the point of having this: to stop arguing about every purchase because there's already a place where you can see them all.

## After 30 days

When you have a full month, you have what you were missing: **your own numbers**.

That's when you open Budget, and instead of inventing how much you should spend on food, you look at how much you actually spent and decide from there. A budget built like that holds, because it's born from your real life, not an ideal.

You'll be able to answer the three questions from the start. And the third one — _what do I want to change?_ — will finally have a concrete answer instead of a feeling.

## An important disclaimer

We are not certified financial advisors and this is not financial advice. What you find here are general, widely accepted ideas on how to start tracking and understanding your spending.

For decisions specific to your situation — debts, loans, investments, business decisions, tax matters — seek a professional who can look at your numbers and do the math for your case. This guide helps you arrive at that conversation with organized information, which is already a lot.
