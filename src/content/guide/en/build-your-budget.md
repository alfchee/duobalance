# Building a budget from real numbers

The [starter guide](/guide/starter-guide) asked you to ignore the Budget tab for two to four weeks. **That wait ends today.** You now have what you were missing: a month of your real numbers, which you learned to read in the [previous lesson](/guide/read-your-first-month). Five minutes to turn them into a budget that holds.

---

## Why this budget holds

An invented budget is a wish. You decide you will spend C$6,000 on food without ever measuring what you spend, and by week two it is broken. You did not fail: the method failed. Nobody sticks to a number that came out of nowhere.

Yours comes from somewhere else. You already spent C$9,500 on food last month, so setting a C$9,000 limit is not dreaming: it is describing your real life with a small adjustment. And a small adjustment can actually be kept. That is the whole difference: a budget born from your records survives; one born from your intentions does not.

---

## From actual spending to a limit, step by step

Open the **Budget** tab. If you have no budgets yet and already recorded at least ten expenses, the app meets you with a proposal: a prompt offering to create budgets from your actual spending, with suggested amounts based on what you spent in the last 30 days. If you have no spending recorded in that window yet, the suggestion uses your earlier transactions instead.

Those amounts come from no formula: they are what you spent, category by category. They arrive pre-filled and you can edit each one before saving — that adjustment is, literally, your budget.

To do it by hand — or to add a category later — use the option to add a budget: pick the category and set its monthly limit. Every budget has a scope: **Household** for shared spending or **Mine** for personal spending.

---

## Change two or three categories, not all of them

The classic first-budget mistake is trying to fix everything at once: seven new limits, all below actual spending. Every category you change demands attention each week, and attention runs out. By mid-month nobody is looking at anything.

Pick **two or three categories** where change is worth it. Usually those are the biggest variable ones, as you saw in the [previous lesson](/guide/read-your-first-month). With that lesson's numbers it would look like this:

- Food: you spent C$9,500 → C$9,000 limit. A little less, not half.
- Transport: you spent C$2,400 → C$2,200 limit.
- Home: you spent C$11,800 → C$11,800 limit. It is fixed and does not move week to week, so its limit is just a guardrail.

The rest of the categories get limits too — set to what you spent — so nothing moves without you seeing it. Changing little is not being timid: it is what keeps the budget alive three months from now.

## The famous rules, honestly

At some point you will run into the 50/30/20 rule: 50% to needs, 30% to wants, 20% to savings. Use it for what it is: **a reference to compare against, not a grade**.

Frankly: those rules assume a stable income that covers needs with room to spare. That is not the reality of many households in the region, where incomes are low or shift month to month. If C$26,000 comes in and Home + Food + Transport alone add up to C$23,700, then 50% for needs (C$13,000) does not describe your life — and it does not have to. With irregular income, the same percentage also means different money each month, so the rule moves under your feet.

Hold yourself up to that mirror if it helps, but decide with your numbers. Your real month is worth more than any imported percentage.

## When the month goes over

It will happen, probably in the first month. In the **Budget** tab you will see it stated without drama: the category shows how much it went over. That is information, not a scolding. A budget does not break because a month goes over. It breaks when you stop looking at it.

When it happens, four steps:

- **Do not move everything mid-month.** A panic adjustment lasts less than the month does.
- **Write down where it went.** One line is enough: food went C$800 over because of two birthdays. Next month that note is gold.
- **Decide one thing for next month.** Either raise the limit, because your actual spending told you it was low, or change the habit, because the spending tells you where. Just one.
- **Keep tracking.** The month that goes over and gets recorded teaches. The one that goes over and gets abandoned does not.

If an irregular category like health usually closes with money left, you can carry the remainder into next month.

---

## Review once a month, not every day

Checking the budget every day is like weighing yourself three times a day: noise that creates anxiety and no decisions. A budget gets reviewed **once a month**, with the closed month in front of you.

The tab lets you move between months and copy the previous month's budgets, adjusting all amounts by a percentage if everything went up. Ten minutes a month is enough: what went over, what was left, what changes next month. As a couple, that is the monthly conversation from the [starter guide](/guide/starter-guide): ten minutes, numbers in view, zero arguments per purchase.

## An important note

We are not certified financial advisers and this is not financial advice. These are general, widely accepted ideas about building and using a budget.

For decisions specific to your situation — debt, loans, investments, business, taxes — see a qualified professional who can look at your numbers. Arriving at that conversation with a budget based on your actual spending is already an advantage.
